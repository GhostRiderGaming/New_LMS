Modes of Transmission

Pathogens utilize different vehicles and vectors to move from reservoir to host.

1

Droplet Transmission

Large respiratory particles (>5μm) traveling short distances (sneezing, coughing).

2

Airborne Transmission

Aerosolized particles (<5μm) suspended in air for long periods, travelling far.

3

Direct Contact

Physical transfer via skin-to-skin contact or kissing.

4

Vector-borne

Living organisms (mosquitoes, ticks) carrying pathogens between hosts.

5

Vehicle-borne (Fomites)

Inanimate objects, contaminated water, or food spreading the disease.

The Chain of Infection

Transmission is a sequential process. Breaking any single link halts the spread of the disease.

1. Infectious Agent

The pathogen (virus, bacteria, fungus) with characteristics like virulence and infectious dose.

2. Reservoir

The environment where the agent lives and multiplies (human, animal, water).

3. Portal of Exit

The path by which the pathogen leaves the reservoir (respiratory tract, blood).

4. Mode of Transmission

The mechanism of transfer (droplet, airborne, vector) bridging the gap.

5. Portal of Entry

How the agent enters the new host (inhalation, broken skin, ingestion).

6. Susceptible Host

A person lacking immunity. Once infected, they become the new Reservoir, restarting the cycle.

Epidemiology Fundamentals

R0 (R-Naught)

The basic reproduction number indicates how contagious a disease is. An R0 of 2 means one infected person will, on average, infect two others in a fully susceptible population.

Herd Immunity

When a large portion of a community becomes immune (via infection or vaccination), the spread is unlikely, providing indirect protection to those who aren't immune.

Common Myth

"Viruses always evolve to become less deadly." While often true as dead hosts don't spread disease, evolution only optimizes for transmission. If high viral load increases spread, virulence may remain high.

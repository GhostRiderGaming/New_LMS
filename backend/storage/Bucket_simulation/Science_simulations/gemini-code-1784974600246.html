1. Optical Components of the Eye

The eye is a complex biological camera. Hover or tap the markers to explore the structures responsible for focusing light.

1
Cornea Clear front window. Provides 70% of the eye's focusing power by bending incoming light.
2
Sclera The tough, white outer coat that maintains the shape of the eyeball and protects internal structures.
3
Crystalline Lens Flexible biconvex structure. Changes shape to fine-tune focus for near or distant objects (Accommodation).
4
Iris & Pupil Colored muscle that controls the pupil size, regulating how much light enters the eye.
5
Retina Light-sensitive screen at the back. Contains photoreceptors (rods and cones) that convert light to signals.
6
Optic Nerve Bundle of nerve fibers transmitting visual information from the retina to the brain.

Presbyopia (Aging Eye)

Around age 40, the crystalline lens loses elasticity. Even without myopia/hyperopia, the eye struggles to accommodate for near vision, requiring "reading glasses" (convex lenses).

📏

Astigmatism

Occurs when the cornea is shaped more like a football than a basketball. This causes light to focus at multiple points, resulting in blurred or distorted vision at all distances. Corrected with cylindrical lenses.

✨

Laser Eye Surgery (LASIK)

Instead of placing a lens outside the eye, lasers precisely vaporize microscopic amounts of corneal tissue, permanently altering its curve to achieve the perfect focal point internally.

Quick Revision Reference

Defect Name Common Name Physical Cause Focal Point Corrective Lens Lens Function
Myopia Nearsightedness Eyeball too long or cornea too curved. In front of retina Concave (diverging) Spreads light rays out before they enter eye, pushing focus back.
Hypermetropia Farsightedness Eyeball too short or cornea too flat. Behind retina Convex (converging) Bends light rays inward before they enter eye, pulling focus forward.

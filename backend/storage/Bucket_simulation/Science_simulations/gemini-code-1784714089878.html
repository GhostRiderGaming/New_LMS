Mechanism: How It Works

An earthquake is not a single moment, but a linear sequence of energy accumulation and sudden release. Watch the stages.

1|Stress Build-up

Tectonic forces push rocks in opposite directions. Friction prevents movement, causing elastic energy to build up over years.

Types of Seismic Waves

Energy travels through rock in different ways. Notice how the particle motion (dots) differs from the wave direction (arrow).

P-Wave (Primary)

Fastest (~6 km/s)

Compressional wave. Particles push and pull in the same direction the wave travels. Travels through solid and liquid.

➔ Direction

S-Wave (Secondary)

Slower (~3.5 km/s)

Shear wave. Particles move up and down, perpendicular to wave direction. Travels only through solids.

➔ Direction

Rayleigh Wave (Surface)

Slowest, Most Damage

Rolls along the surface like an ocean wave. Particles move in elliptical paths. Causes complex ground shaking.

Love Wave (Surface)

Horizontal Shear

Moves the surface of the earth side-to-side horizontally. Extremely damaging to building foundations.

Interesting Fact

The Earth is completely "liquid" to S-waves in its outer core. Because S-waves cannot travel through liquids, they create massive "shadow zones" on the opposite side of the Earth from an earthquake, which is how we discovered the liquid outer core!

❌
Common Mistake

People often confuse Magnitude with Intensity. Magnitude (Richter/Moment scale) measures total energy released at the source (one number per quake). Intensity (Mercalli scale) measures the shaking effect at a specific location (changes based on distance).

🏗️
Application

Engineers use shake tables to simulate surface waves (Rayleigh/Love) on building models. To counter resonance, modern skyscrapers use "Tuned Mass Dampers"—giant pendulums that sway in the opposite direction of the building during a quake.

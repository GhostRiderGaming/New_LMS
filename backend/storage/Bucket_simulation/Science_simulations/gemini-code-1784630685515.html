Chemical Applications

Matching Agent to Combustion Type

Using the wrong extinguisher can spread a fire or cause electrocution. Different chemical agents are engineered to break the fire tetrahedron in specific ways.

Water & Foam

Removes the HEAT element. Water absorbs thermal energy rapidly due to high latent heat of vaporization.

Fights: Class A (Wood, Paper, Cloth)
Danger: Never use on electrical or oil fires.

Carbon Dioxide (CO2)

Removes the OXYGEN element. Displaces O2 around the fire and provides some cooling (discharges at extremely low temps).

Fights: Class B (Liquids), Class C/E (Electrical)
Danger: Asphyxiation risk in confined spaces.

Dry Chemical Powder

Interrupts the CHEMICAL REACTION. Fine powder coats the fuel, separating it from oxygen and halting chain reactions.

Fights: Class A, B, C (Highly versatile ABC powder)
Danger: Obscures vision, damages sensitive electronics.

Wet Chemical

Causes SAPONIFICATION. Reacts with hot cooking oil to create a soapy, non-combustible crust that seals off oxygen.

Fights: Class K / F (Cooking Oils and Fats)
Benefit: The only effective agent for deep fat fryers.

Fascinating Physics

Extreme Pressure

A standard CO2 extinguisher stores gas at approximately 830 PSI (57 bar) at room temperature. For comparison, a car tire is about 32 PSI.

Dry Ice Snow

When CO2 is released, the rapid expansion (Joule-Thomson effect) drops the temperature so drastically that it exits the horn as "dry ice" snow at -78.5°C (-109.3°F).

Fatal Mistakes

Aiming at the Flames

Amateurs aim at the top of the flames (the plume). The agent is wasted in the thermal updraft. You must aim at the base where the fuel is actually vaporizing.

Water on Oil Fires

Water is heavier than oil. It sinks to the bottom of the pan, instantly turns to steam, expands 1,700 times its volume, and violently erupts a fireball of burning oil into the room.

Quick Revision

Pull Pin → Aim Base → Squeeze Lever → Sweep Side-to-Side.
Pressure forces agent up the siphon tube. Agent removes Heat, Oxygen, or Chemical Chain Reaction.

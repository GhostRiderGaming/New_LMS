Overview

A gas station built from soap bubbles

Each alveolus is a microscopic air sac wrapped in a net of capillaries. Air on one side, blood on the other, separated by a wall so thin that gases simply drift across it — no pumping required, just physics doing the work.

What actually moves

Oxygen (O₂) travels from the air in the alveolus into the blood, because there's more of it in the air than in the blood. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) travels the opposite way — from blood into air — for the same reason: it simply moves from where it's crowded to where it's scarce. This one-way-each rule is called diffusion.

Why the design works so well

Millions of tiny sacs instead of two big balloons means far more surface area for gas to cross. A membrane only one cell thick on each side means gases barely have to travel. And a dense capillary web means blood is always right there, ready to trade.

Applications

Where this shows up in real life

High-altitude climbing

Thinner air at altitude means fewer O₂ molecules per breath, so diffusion slows down — which is why climbers carry supplemental oxygen above certain heights.

Pulse oximeters

The clip-on sensor used in hospitals — and by millions during respiratory illness — shines light through a fingertip to estimate blood oxygen saturation in real time.

Smoking & emphysema

Smoke damages and merges alveolar walls, shrinking total surface area — the lungs still work, just with far less room to trade gases.

Exercise response

Working muscles produce more CO₂ and demand more O₂, so breathing rate rises automatically to keep the exchange matched to demand.

Pneumonia

Fluid filling the alveoli physically blocks the air side of the membrane, which is why pneumonia causes breathlessness even though the lungs are still expanding normally.

Scuba diving

Pressure changes underwater alter how gases dissolve in blood, which is why divers ascend slowly — to let gas exchange keep pace safely.

Interesting Facts

Small sacs, big numbers

70 m²

Unfolded, the total surface area of all your alveoli combined is roughly the size of a tennis court — packed inside two organs you can hold in your hands.

~16%

The air you exhale still contains about 16% oxygen, which is exactly why rescue breathing during CPR can keep someone alive.

0.75s

A red blood cell typically spends about 0.75 seconds passing through a lung capillary, but only needs about a third of that to fully load with oxygen.

Never

Your lungs never fully empty, even after the hardest exhale — a reserve of air always stays behind so the alveoli don't collapse.

Common Mistakes

Myth vs. reality

✕
Myth

Lungs actively "pump" air like a heart pumps blood.

✓
Reality

The diaphragm changes chest pressure; air simply flows in and out passively, following the pressure difference.

✕
Myth

Gas exchange happens throughout the whole airway, including the trachea.

✓
Reality

Exchange only happens in alveoli — everywhere else the walls are far too thick for diffusion.

✕
Myth

Exhaled air has no oxygen left in it at all.

✓
Reality

Exhaled air still holds roughly 16% oxygen — well above the ~4% typically absorbed on a single breath.

✕
Myth

"Breathing" and "respiration" mean the same thing.

✓
Reality

Breathing is the mechanical movement of air; respiration is how cells use oxygen to release energy from food.

Archimedes' Principle

Why do giant steel ships float while tiny pebbles sink? It all comes down to a fundamental law of physics discovered over 2,000 years ago. Discover how fluids exert an upward force on objects.

Fb = ρ × V × g

Buoyant Force = Fluid Density × Submerged Volume × Gravity

The Classic Experiment

To understand the principle visually, we use an overflow can experiment. It proves that the upward buoyant force is exactly equal to the weight of the fluid pushed out of the way.

Real Application: Steel Ships

Steel is 8 times denser than water. Why does a cruise ship float? It's shaped like a bowl containing mostly air. The average density of the ship (steel + air inside) is less than water, allowing it to displace a massive volume of water to equal its weight.

🎈

Real Application: Hot Air Balloons

Archimedes' principle applies to gases too! Air is a fluid. By heating the air inside the envelope, it expands and becomes less dense. It displaces heavier cold air outside, creating an upward buoyant force greater than gravity.

❌

Common Mistake

"Buoyancy increases the deeper you go."

Once an object is fully submerged, buoyant force remains constant (assuming water is incompressible). It depends entirely on displaced volume, which stops increasing once fully underwater.

Quick Revision

The core takeaways of Archimedes' Principle:

  • Any object immersed in a fluid experiences an upward force (Buoyancy).
  • Magnitude: Buoyant Force = Weight of displaced fluid.
  • Floating: If Object Density < Fluid Density, it floats. ($F_b$ can equal $F_g$).
  • Sinking: If Object Density > Fluid Density, it sinks. ($F_b$ max is less than $F_g$).
  • Apparent Weight: An object feels lighter in water (Actual Weight - Buoyant Force).

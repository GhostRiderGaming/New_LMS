Topic Overview

One star, eight worlds, one shared set of rules

The Solar System is the Sun and everything bound to it by gravity: eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, and comets. Every object obeys the same two rules — gravity pulls it inward, motion carries it forward — and the balance between those two forces is what turns a straight line into an orbit.

Applications

Why orbital mechanics matters

The same physics you just simulated keeps real hardware and real calendars running.

GPS & Satellites

Engineers use the same gravity-versus-inertia balance to keep GPS and communication satellites in stable, predictable orbits.

Calendars & Seasons

A year is one full Earth orbit; a day is one full Earth spin. Leap years exist because those two cycles don't divide evenly.

Space Missions

Missions like Voyager and Mars rovers ride "gravity assists" — slingshotting past a planet to gain speed for free.

Tides

The Moon's and Sun's gravity pull on Earth's oceans, creating the twice-daily rise and fall of the tides.

Eclipse Prediction

Because orbits are stable and repeating, astronomers can predict eclipses centuries in advance to the minute.

Navigation

Sailors and pilots historically fixed their position using the measured angles of the Sun, Moon, and stars.

Quick Revision

The whole system, at a glance

  • The Sun holds everything together with gravity and supplies almost all the system's mass and light.
  • Every orbit is a balance between gravity pulling inward and inertia carrying the planet forward.
  • Orbits are technically ellipses, not perfect circles — planets move faster near the Sun, slower far away.
  • Inner planets are small and rocky; outer planets are large and gaseous or icy.
  • Nothing here is drawn to true scale — real distances are far too vast to fit on one screen.

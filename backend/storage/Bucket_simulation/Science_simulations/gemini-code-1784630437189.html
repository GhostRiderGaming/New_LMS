Structure of a Flame

A flame is not a single entity. It consists of distinct zones based on oxygen availability and temperature.

1
2

Non-Luminous Zone

The outermost layer. Oxygen supply is abundant here, leading to complete combustion. It produces very little light but the most heat.

Complete Combustion Hottest Part (~1400°C)
2

Luminous Zone

The middle yellow area. Limited oxygen causes incomplete combustion. Unburnt carbon particles get heated up and glow brightly (incandescence), producing the yellow light.

Incomplete Combustion Moderate Heat (~1000°C)
3

Dark Zone

The innermost area surrounding the wick. It contains unburnt wax vapors because oxygen cannot reach this deep inside the flame.

No Combustion Least Hot (~800°C)

Why is it yellow?

The yellow light doesn't come from a gas burning. It comes from solid soot (carbon particles) heating up until they glow red, then yellow, exactly like the filament in an old lightbulb.

🧲

Zero Gravity Flame

In space, a candle flame is completely round and blue! Without gravity, hot air doesn't rise (no convection), so the teardrop shape never forms and oxygen only reaches the surface slowly via diffusion.

⚠️

Common Mistake

People often think the brightest part of the flame is the hottest. It's actually the opposite. The faint, barely visible blue outer edge is where combustion is complete and temperature is highest.

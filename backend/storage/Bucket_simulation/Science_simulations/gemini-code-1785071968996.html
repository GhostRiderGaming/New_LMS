Anatomy of an Echo

An echo requires specific elements to occur. The reflecting surface must be large, rigid, and far enough away for the sound to travel back after a slight delay.

Sound bouncing back. Some energy is lost, so the echo is always quieter than the original.

Receiver & Brain

The ear detects the return wave. The brain needs a minimum ~0.1s delay to distinguish it from the original sound.

Applications

SONAR

Ships use Sound Navigation and Ranging. They emit ultrasound pulses into water and measure the time taken for echoes to return from the seabed or submarines to calculate depth.

Medical Ultrasound

High-frequency sound waves enter the body. Different tissues reflect sound differently. The returning echoes are mapped by a computer to create real-time internal images.

Animal Echolocation

Bats and dolphins navigate and hunt in total darkness by emitting clicks and analyzing the echoes to determine the size, distance, and speed of objects.

Facts & Misconceptions

The 17 Meter Minimum

At room temperature (20°C), sound travels at ~343 m/s. To hear an echo (0.1s delay), sound must travel at least 34.3 meters total. Therefore, the reflecting wall must be at least 17.15 meters away.

Mistake: Echo is a new sound

Correction: An echo is not a new sound created by the wall. It is the exact same mechanical energy that left the source, bouncing back like a tennis ball hitting a wall.

Mistake: Reverberation is just a fast echo

Correction: While physically similar, reverberation involves multiple, rapid reflections blending together, prolonging the sound (like in an empty hall). A true echo is a distinct, separate repetition.

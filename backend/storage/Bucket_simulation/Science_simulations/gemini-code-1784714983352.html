The Anatomy of an Orbit

An orbit is the gravitationally curved trajectory of an object. To understand how planets move, we must first identify the structural components of their path.

Primary Focus (Star)

The massive body being orbited. It sits at one focal point of the ellipse, not the exact center.

2

Orbital Body (Planet)

Moving continuously due to the balance of its forward velocity (blue) and gravitational pull (red).

3

Perihelion

The point in the orbit closest to the star. The planet travels fastest here.

4

Aphelion

The point farthest from the star. Gravitational pull is weakest, so velocity is slowest.

5

Semi-Major Axis

Half of the longest diameter of the ellipse. Determines the orbital period.

Real-world Application

Satellite Deployment: Engineers use orbital mechanics to place GPS, weather, and communication satellites into exact orbits. Geostationary orbits match Earth's rotation perfectly.

Common Mistake

"There is no gravity in space."

Truth: There is a lot of gravity! Astronauts on the ISS float because they are in continuous freefall (orbiting), not because gravity is zero. Earth's gravity at ISS altitude is still ~90% as strong as on the surface.

Interesting Fact

Slingshot Maneuver: Spacecraft steal a tiny fraction of a planet's orbital momentum when flying closely past it (Gravity Assist), significantly boosting their speed towards deep space.

Anatomy of a Seismograph

A traditional horizontal pendulum seismograph relies on inertia. While the earth moves, the heavy mass tries to remain perfectly still.

1

Support Frame

Anchored firmly to the bedrock. Moves exactly as the earth shakes.

2

Heavy Mass (Pendulum)

Due to high inertia, it resists motion and stays stationary while the frame moves around it.

3

Suspension System

Wire and hinge that allow the mass to hang freely with minimal friction.

4

Stylus / Pen

Attached to the stationary mass. Records relative movement onto the paper.

5

Rotating Drum

Driven by a clockwork motor. Provides a continuous timeline (X-axis) for the data.

6

Bedrock Anchor

Direct connection to the earth's crust to capture authentic seismic waves.

P-Waves vs S-Waves

Primary (P) waves travel faster and arrive first, causing a small initial jiggle. Secondary (S) waves are slower but cause massive up-and-down shaking, producing the large spikes on the seismogram.

The "Still" Mass Fallacy

A common mistake is thinking the mass remains perfectly still forever. In reality, a damper (like a magnet or oil bath) is used to slowly bring the mass back to center, preventing it from swinging indefinitely like a grandfather clock.

Modern Evolution

Today's seismometers don't use heavy physical masses and pens. They use micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) or force-feedback coils where electronic sensors measure the tiny amount of electricity needed to hold a small mass in place.

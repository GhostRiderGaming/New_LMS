Anatomy of Momentum

Momentum (p) is a vector quantity, meaning it has both magnitude and direction. It is the product of an object's mass and its velocity.

1
2
3
1
Mass (m) The amount of matter in the object. Acts as resistance to changes in motion (inertia). Measured in kg.
2
Velocity (v) The speed of the object in a specific direction. Vectors indicate where it's heading. Measured in m/s.
3
Momentum Trail (p) The resulting "quantity of motion". Calculated as p = m × v. Measured in kg·m/s.
Formula: p = m * v

Real-World Applications

🚀

Rocket Propulsion

In space, there is nothing to push against. Rockets move forward by expelling high-velocity gas backwards. To conserve the system's total momentum (initially zero), the rocket must acquire forward momentum equal to the exhaust's backward momentum.

🎱

Billiards & Sports

When a cue ball strikes a stationary ball dead-center (and both have equal mass), the cue ball stops completely, transferring 100% of its momentum and kinetic energy to the target ball. This is a near-perfect elastic collision.

🚗

Car Airbags (Impulse)

Impulse is the change in momentum ($J = F \times \Delta t$). To stop a person's momentum during a crash, an airbag increases the time ($\Delta t$) of impact. By increasing time, the peak Force ($F$) applied to the body is drastically reduced, saving lives.

Quick Revision: Common Mistakes

  • Confusing Velocity with Speed: Momentum relies on velocity (which has direction). If two identical cars crash head-on at the same speed, their total system momentum is zero ($p + (-p) = 0$), not doubled.
  • Ignoring Mass: A bullet has high velocity but low mass. A moving ship has low velocity but massive mass. The ship has vastly more momentum.
  • Kinetic Energy vs Momentum: Momentum is ALWAYS conserved in a closed system. Kinetic energy is ONLY conserved in perfectly elastic collisions (like bouncy balls), but lost as heat/sound in inelastic ones (like car crashes).

The Physics of a Rainbow

💧 Anatomy of the Phenomenon

A rainbow isn't a physical object, but an optical illusion created by the interaction of light and water. Here are the core components required for its formation.

1

Incident Sunlight

White light from the sun, containing all visible wavelengths, travels in a straight line toward the droplet.

2

Air-Water Interface

Light enters the denser water, slowing down. It bends (refracts) and begins to split into colors (dispersion).

3

Back Boundary

Light hits the inside back of the drop. Most of it undergoes Total Internal Reflection, bouncing forward.

4

Exit Point

Light exits back into the air. It refracts a second time, widening the gap between the different colors.

5

Observer's Eye

Specific colors reach the eye at specific angles (~40° to 42°), creating the perception of bands in the sky.

🧠 Beyond the Basics

🌈

Double Rainbows

Occur when light reflects twice inside the raindrop before exiting. This secondary reflection reverses the color order (violet on top, red on bottom) and makes it dimmer.

⭕

They are actually Circles

From the ground, the horizon cuts off the bottom half of a rainbow. If you view a rainbow from an airplane, you can see the complete 360-degree circular rainbow.

👁️

Personal Illusion

Because the angles depend on the observer's exact position, no two people see the exact same rainbow. You are the apex of the visual cone creating it.

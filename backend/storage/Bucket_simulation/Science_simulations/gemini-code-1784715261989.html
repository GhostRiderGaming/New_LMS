Anatomy of an Orbit

An orbit is not a place devoid of gravity. It is a trajectory where an object moves forward so fast that as it falls towards the Earth, the Earth curves away beneath it. It is an act of perpetual freefall.

Gravitational Source

The central massive body (Earth). Its gravity pulls everything towards its center.

2

Payload (Satellite)

The object in motion. In orbital physics, its mass is irrelevant to the shape of the orbit.

3

Tangential Velocity

Forward momentum. Must be precise to prevent falling back or escaping into space.

4

Centripetal Force

Gravity constantly pulling the satellite inward, bending its straight path into a curve.

5

Resultant Orbit

The continuous path formed by combining forward velocity and inward gravitational pull.

Applications

LEO (Low Earth Orbit): 400-2000km. Fast orbital periods (~90 mins). Used for ISS, Hubble, and Starlink internet.

GEO (Geostationary): 35,786km. Orbital period matches Earth's rotation (24 hrs), appearing fixed in the sky. Perfect for weather and TV satellites.

Common Misconceptions

"There is zero gravity in space"

False. At the ISS altitude (400km), gravity is still ~90% as strong as on the surface. Astronauts float because they are in continuous freefall, not because gravity disappeared.

"Heavier satellites fall faster"

In a vacuum, mass does not affect orbital trajectory. A feather and a space station require the exact same velocity to maintain the same orbit.

Fascinating Facts

The International Space Station (ISS) travels at 28,000 km/h (17,500 mph). At this speed, it orbits the Earth once every 92 minutes, meaning astronauts experience 16 sunrises and sunsets every single day.

If you drove a car at highway speeds straight up, it would take nearly 6 months to reach Geostationary orbit.

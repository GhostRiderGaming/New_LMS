Heating Effect of Current

When electric current flows through a conductor, it encounters resistance, converting electrical energy directly into heat. This is known as Joule Heating.

The Macro View: A Basic Circuit

A typical circuit demonstrating this effect consists of a power source, low-resistance connecting wires, and a high-resistance heating element.

Simulation: Joule's Law

James Prescott Joule discovered that the heat generated is proportional to the square of the current, the resistance, and the time. Experiment with the variables below.

Current (I) 2 A

Has the biggest impact (squared).

Resistance (R) 5 Ω

Depends on material and thickness.

Time (t) 10 s

Duration the circuit is closed.

H = I² × R × t
Generated Heat: 200 Joules

Real-World Applications

Electric Iron

Uses a mica-insulated Nichrome wire coil. The high resistance generates intense heat, which is transferred to the heavy metal base for pressing clothes.

Incandescent Bulb

A very thin Tungsten filament offers such high resistance that it heats up to ~2500°C. At this extreme temperature, it emits visible light (incandescence).

Safety Fuse

Made of an alloy with a low melting point. If current exceeds a safe limit, $I^2R$ heating melts the wire instantly, breaking the circuit and preventing fires.

Interesting Fact

Superconductors, when cooled near absolute zero, have exactly zero electrical resistance (R=0). Because $H = I^2Rt$, if R is zero, Heat is zero! Current can flow endlessly through a superconducting loop without losing any energy to heat.

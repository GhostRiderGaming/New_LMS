Relative Density

Why does a massive steel ship float, while a tiny pebble sinks? It's not about weight. It's about how closely packed the material's particles are compared to water.

RD = Density of ObjectDensity of Water

The Variables of Comparison

Relative density is a ratio. It compares the density of a substance to the density of a reference material (universally, pure water at 4°C). Because it's a ratio dividing density by density, it has no units.

1

1

Density of Substance

Mass per unit volume of the object ($\rho = m/V$). Visually: How tightly packed the atoms are in a specific space.

2

Density of Reference (Water)

Pure water has a density of exactly $1000 \text{ kg/m}^3$ (or $1 \text{ g/cm}^3$). This provides our baseline of "1.0".

3

The Comparison (Ratio)

By comparing equal volumes, if the substance is heavier than the water, its RD > 1. If lighter, RD < 1.

Real-World Applications

🔋

The Hydrometer

A sealed glass tube with a weight at the bottom. It floats higher in denser liquids. Used to check battery acid density or test if milk has been diluted with water.

🚢

Ship Design (Plimsoll Line)

Ships are made of steel (RD ~7.8), but they float because they are hollow. Their average density is less than water. The Plimsoll line on the hull shows maximum safe loading depending on water density (salt vs fresh).

🌊

The Dead Sea

Because it is incredibly salty, Dead Sea water has an RD of ~1.24 (compared to pure water). The human body has an RD of ~0.98. The larger difference makes humans float effortlessly.

❌

Common Mistake

"Heavy things sink, light things float." False. A 10,000-ton aircraft carrier floats, while a 1-gram pebble sinks. It is strictly about Density (Mass/Volume), not Mass alone.

Universal Gravitation

The invisible force that shapes the cosmos. It holds galaxies together, keeps planets in orbit, and dictates the fall of every object.

The Universal Law

In 1687, Isaac Newton published a revolutionary idea: every particle in the universe attracts every other particle. He defined exactly how strong that pull is with a single elegant equation.

F = G × ( m₁ × m₂ ) / r²
Force = Constant × (Mass 1 × Mass 2) / Distance Squared

Relative Units

Application: Satellites

Every GPS, weather, and communication satellite relies on precise gravitational calculations to stay in continuous freefall around the Earth without burning up in the atmosphere.

Application: Ocean Tides

The moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans. Because the gravitational force is stronger on the side facing the moon, the water bulges outward, creating high tides.

Fact: You Attract the Earth

When you drop an apple, the Earth pulls it down. But Newton's Third Law means the apple pulls the Earth UP with the exact same amount of force. The Earth's immense mass just means it doesn't move noticeably.

Mistake: "Zero Gravity" in Space

Astronauts float in the ISS not because there is no gravity (gravity at that altitude is still ~90% as strong as on the surface), but because they, and the station, are falling sideways so fast they constantly miss the Earth. It's microgravity (freefall), not zero gravity.

Quick Revision

Gravity is a universal force of attraction.

It increases if the masses increase.
It weakens rapidly if the distance increases (squared).
Orbits are achieved by traveling sideways fast enough that the curve of your fall matches the curve of the planet.

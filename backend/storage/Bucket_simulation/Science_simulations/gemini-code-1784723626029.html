The Rusting Process

Rust isn't just metal getting old. It is a violent, invisible electrochemical war fought at the atomic level between iron, oxygen, and water.

Anatomy of Corrosion

Rusting requires a specific environment to occur. It is an electrochemical cell created on the surface of the metal.

1

Anodic Region

Where Iron (Fe) atoms lose electrons and dissolve into the water as Fe²⁺ ions.

2

Electrolyte (Water)

Water acts as a medium allowing ions to move. Dissolved salts make this faster.

3

Cathodic Region

Edges of the drop where water meets air. Electrons arrive here through the metal.

4

Oxygen (O₂)

Absorbs electrons at the cathode, combining with water to form Hydroxide (OH⁻) ions.

5

Rust (Fe₂O₃·nH₂O)

Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ ions meet, precipitate, and oxidize further to form flaky brown rust.

The Equations

Rusting is a redox reaction. Oxidation happens at the anode, reduction at the cathode.

Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻
🛡️

Galvanization

Coating iron with Zinc protects it even if scratched. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it sacrifices itself. It acts as the anode, forcing iron to act as the cathode, which prevents iron from oxidizing.

🧂

Why Salt Speeds It Up

Saltwater causes faster rusting than pure water. Pure water is a poor conductor of electricity. Salt provides ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻) that make water a great electrolyte, allowing electrons and ions to flow much faster.

Anatomy of a Mirror

Before tracing light, we must understand the geometric framework of a spherical mirror. Every curved mirror is a section of an imaginary hollow sphere.

Pole (P)

The geometric center of the reflecting surface.

2

Center of Curvature (C)

The center of the imaginary sphere the mirror is cut from.

3

Principal Focus (F)

Where parallel rays converge (or appear to diverge from) after reflection.

4

Principal Axis

The imaginary line passing through the Pole and Center of Curvature.

5

Focal Length (f)

Distance between Pole and Focus. Exactly half the Radius of Curvature (R/2).

Rules of Ray Tracing

To predict how an image forms, we trace the path of specific incident rays. The intersection of reflected rays determines the image position.

1. Parallel Ray

A ray traveling parallel to the principal axis reflects exactly through the Principal Focus (F).

Real-World Engineering

Concave

Astronomical Telescopes

Large concave mirrors gather faint starlight and converge it to a brilliant focal point, allowing us to peer into deep space without the color distortion of heavy glass lenses.

Concave

Headlights & Torches

A lightbulb placed exactly at the Principal Focus (F) of a concave reflector produces a powerful, parallel beam of light reaching far distances.

Convex

Rear-View Mirrors

Convex mirrors are used in vehicles because they always form an erect, diminished image. This radically expands the driver's field of view to see traffic behind.

Convex

Security Surveillance

Large domes in store ceilings use convex geometry to allow a single camera or observer to monitor entire aisles by compressing a wide area into a small virtual image.
